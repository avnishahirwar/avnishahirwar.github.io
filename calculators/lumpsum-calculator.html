Guide to the Lumpsum Calculator: method, example and FAQs

How a lumpsum investment grows

A lumpsum is a single deposit made today and left alone. There are no monthly instalments, so the whole result comes from one thing: compounding. Each year the interest or return is added to the balance, and the next year’s return is earned on that larger balance. The formula this calculator uses is Future value = P × (1 + r)n, where P is the amount invested, r is the expected annual return as a decimal and n is the number of years.

Take ₹1,00,000 invested at an assumed 12% a year for 10 years. The multiplier is 1.1210 = 3.1058, so the maturity value is ₹3,10,585 and the gain is ₹2,10,585. More than half of that gain arrives in the last four years, which is why starting early matters far more than choosing a slightly higher rate.

A quick mental check: the rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 12% that is about 6 years, and the exact figure is 6.1 years. At 6% it is about 12 years. If the calculator shows a result that contradicts this rule by a wide margin, re-check that you entered the rate as a yearly percentage and the period in years.

Lumpsum versus SIP

A lumpsum puts the full amount to work from day one, which gives it the highest ending value when markets rise steadily. A SIP spreads the same money over months, which lowers the risk of investing everything just before a fall. If you already hold the money, the real choice is between investing it at once and staggering it over 6 to 12 months; if you earn a salary, a SIP is the natural fit. Use the SIP calculator on this site to compare both on your numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Treating an assumed return as a promise: equity funds do not earn a fixed rate and a fixed deposit does, so the same formula means different things for each.
  • Ignoring tax and inflation: a 12% nominal return with 6% inflation is roughly a 5.7% real return, because (1.12 ÷ 1.06) − 1 ≈ 5.7%.
  • Mixing up months and years: entering 120 in the years box gives an absurd answer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for a lumpsum calculation?

Future value = P × (1 + r)^n, where P is the principal, r the annual rate as a decimal and n the number of years. For compounding more often than yearly, use r/m and n × m.

Is the result guaranteed?

No. It is a projection from the rate you type. Fixed deposits and small-savings schemes have declared rates; market-linked investments do not.

How much will ₹1,00,000 become in 10 years at 12%?

About ₹3,10,585, a gain of ₹2,10,585.

Does this include tax?

No. Taxes on gains depend on the instrument and your holding period, so subtract them separately.
