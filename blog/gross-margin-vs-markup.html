Gross Margin vs Markup: Formula, Example and Pricing Use

Original calculator guide · Published 21 November 2026

A product can have a 25% markup and a 20% gross margin at the same time. The difference is not a contradiction; it comes from dividing profit by cost versus selling price. Margin is commonly used to discuss revenue profitability, while markup is often used to build a selling price from a known cost. State the term whenever you quote a percentage.

The formula and the baseline

Gross profit = selling price − cost. Markup = gross profit ÷ cost × 100. Gross margin = gross profit ÷ selling price × 100.

The formula is a model of the relationship between the inputs. It is not a hidden guarantee about a bank, supplier, machine, health outcome or government assessment. Keep the original baseline visible because a result without its baseline can be difficult to audit. When units are changed, convert first and round only after the main calculation.

Worked example

A product costing ₹800 and selling for ₹1,000 has ₹200 gross profit. Markup is 25%, while margin is 20%. This example is intentionally limited: it demonstrates the arithmetic, not a recommendation. Interpretation and decision use

When converting a target margin m into a price, price = cost ÷ (1 − m). That is different from multiplying cost by 1 + markup, so label the input clearly. Use the output to ask a better question: what changes if demand falls, the input cost rises, the time period changes, or the measurement is off by a reasonable amount? Sensitivity is often more valuable than a false impression of precision. If two scenarios are close, the decision may depend on liquidity, safety, quality, tax treatment, service level or contractual terms that are outside this simple calculation.

Common mistakes to avoid

The biggest mistake is applying a target margin as if it were a markup. Also avoid comparing margins before deciding whether costs include freight, discounts, returns and payment charges. Also check whether the calculator's denominator, date range and rounding convention match the document or process you are comparing. Next, run at least three cases. The base case should use the best-supported figures available.
