Compound Interest: How Time and Rate Affect Growth

Compare simple and compound interest and see why compounding frequency and time matter. · Published 13 November 2026

Compound interest means interest is calculated on the original principal and on interest already added to the balance. Over time, this can create a faster increase than simple interest, where interest is calculated only on the original principal. The difference depends on the rate, time and compounding frequency.

1. Simple interest and compound interest

With simple interest, the interest amount is based on the original principal for each period. With compound interest, the accumulated balance becomes the base for later interest calculations.

Simple interest = Principal × Rate × Time ÷ 100
Compound amount = Principal × (1 + rate ÷ n)n × time

In the compound formula, the rate is annual, n is the number of compounding periods per year and time is measured in years. The formula assumes a constant rate and regular compounding.

2. Why compounding frequency matters

For the same nominal annual rate and time, more frequent compounding can produce a slightly higher effective annual yield. The difference is usually smaller than the effect of changing the rate or time horizon. Always check whether the quoted rate is nominal, effective or a product-specific yield.

3. Time can matter as much as the rate

Compounding is cumulative. A longer period gives earlier interest more time to earn additional interest. This is why a small difference in start date or duration can affect the final value, even if the annual rate stays unchanged.

4. Use realistic assumptions

For a bank deposit, use the bank's actual compounding convention, payout option and tenure. For a market-linked investment, a constant-rate compound-interest calculation is only a mathematical illustration; real returns vary and are not guaranteed. Do not confuse a calculator's assumed rate with a promised product return.

Frequently asked questions

Is compound interest always better?

The formula can produce a higher balance than simple interest under the same positive rate and time, but the actual product may involve risk, fees, tax or changing rates.

Does daily compounding always mean a much higher return?

Usually the frequency effect is incremental. Rate, time, product terms and fees may have a larger impact.

Can I use this formula for mutual funds?

Only as a simplified illustration. Mutual-fund returns are market-linked and do not accrue at a fixed guaranteed rate.
