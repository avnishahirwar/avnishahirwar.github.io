SIP and Inflation: How to Understand Real Returns

See the difference between a nominal investment projection and future purchasing power. · Published 13 November 2026

A future investment value can look large in rupees while buying less than the same amount buys today. That difference is the effect of inflation. When reviewing a systematic investment plan (SIP), separate the projected account value from the future purchasing power of that value.

1. Nominal value versus real value

A nominal projection shows the future amount in future rupees. A real-value estimate adjusts that amount for an assumed inflation rate to express its approximate purchasing power in today's rupees. It does not predict market performance; it is a way to compare money across time.

2. A simple inflation illustration

Suppose a goal is expected to cost ₹10 lakh today and general prices rise by an assumed 5% a year for 10 years. The future cost estimate is approximately ₹16.29 lakh. The calculation is ₹10,00,000 × (1.05)10. The actual cost of a specific goal may rise faster or slower than broad inflation.

Future cost = Current cost × (1 + assumed annual inflation)number of years

3. What a SIP projection can and cannot tell you

A SIP calculator combines contribution amount, frequency, duration and an assumed return convention to estimate a future value. It does not know which mutual fund you will choose, future NAV on each instalment date, expense ratios, exit loads or tax treatment unless those items are separately modelled. An assumed return is not a forecast or guarantee.

4. Estimate the real value of a projected corpus

To convert a future amount into today's purchasing-power terms, divide it by (1 + inflation)years. For example, a projected ₹20 lakh after 10 years at 5% assumed inflation has a present-value equivalent of about ₹12.28 lakh. This is an illustration, not a promised return.

Today's purchasing-power estimate = Future amount ÷ (1 + assumed inflation)years

5. Build a goal-based scenario

  1. Estimate the current cost of the goal using a realistic quote or budget.
  2. Choose an inflation assumption and test more than one scenario.
  3. Estimate the future goal cost.
  4. Model a contribution amount and duration in the SIP calculator.
  5. Compare the projected corpus with the future goal cost, leaving room for uncertainty.

6. Use the calculators together

Start with the SIP Calculator to estimate contributions under a stated return assumption. Then use the Inflation Calculator to see how purchasing power changes over time. Review the gap between the nominal projection and future goal cost rather than focusing on a single headline number.

Important: Inflation varies by category and location. Education, healthcare, rent and household costs may not follow the same rate as a broad consumer-price index.

7. Compare several inflation scenarios

One inflation assumption can create a false sense of precision. For a goal costing ₹10 lakh today, a 4% annual increase over 10 years gives a future-cost estimate of about ₹14.80 lakh. At 6%, the estimate is about ₹17.91 lakh. The difference is more than ₹3 lakh even though the assumed annual rates differ by only two percentage points. This is why it is useful to test a range rather than select the most convenient assumption.

8. Match the time horizon to the goal

A short-term goal and a long-term goal should not be evaluated with the same investment assumptions. For a near-term expense, a temporary fall in market value may matter more because there is less time to wait for recovery. For a longer-term goal, inflation and changing contributions may matter more. The appropriate product and risk level depend on the goal, time horizon and personal circumstances; a calculator cannot make that suitability decision.

9. Revisit the plan when inputs change

Update the current cost estimate when you receive a new school fee schedule, rent quote, construction estimate or medical cost. Review contributions when income changes and revisit the time remaining to the goal. Do not increase an assumed investment return just to make the target appear achievable. If the gap remains large, compare a different contribution amount, time horizon or goal cost. For background on mutual-fund risks, read the investor material from AMFI's investor corner.

Frequently asked questions

Does a SIP protect money from inflation?

Not automatically. A SIP is an investment method; the underlying fund can gain or lose value and may or may not outpace inflation.

Is the real return guaranteed?

No. Both investment returns and future inflation are uncertain. The real-value calculation is only as reliable as its assumptions.

Should I use one inflation rate for every goal?

A broad rate is a starting point. Use goal-specific cost information where available, especially for education, housing and healthcare.
