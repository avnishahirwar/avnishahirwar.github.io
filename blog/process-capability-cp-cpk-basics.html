Cp and Cpk: A Practical Process Capability Explanation

Original calculator guide · Published 21 November 2026

Process capability indices compare process spread and centering with specification limits. They are useful only when the measurement system and process are appropriate for the question. A high index does not prove that the process is stable, the specifications are correct or the gauge is reliable. Capability is a statistical estimate tied to the sample and assumptions.

What the calculation measures

A high index does not prove that the process is stable, the specifications are correct or the gauge is reliable. Capability is a statistical estimate tied to the sample and assumptions. A useful estimate begins by naming the quantity, the period or physical boundary, and the unit. That sounds simple, but many mistakes come from entering a correct number into the wrong definition. Before opening the tool, write down what is included and excluded. If the input is a rate, identify its denominator. If it is a cost, decide whether tax, fees, wastage, interest or delivery are included.

The formula and the baseline

Cp = (USL − LSL) ÷ (6σ). Cpk is the smaller of (USL − mean) ÷ (3σ) and (mean − LSL) ÷ (3σ).

The formula is a model of the relationship between the inputs. It is not a hidden guarantee about a bank, supplier, machine, health outcome or government assessment. Keep the original baseline visible because a result without its baseline can be difficult to audit. When units are changed, convert first and round only after the main calculation.

Worked example

With USL 10.6, LSL 9.4, mean 10.1 and σ 0.1, Cp is 2.0 while Cpk is about 1.67 because the mean is not exactly centered. This example is intentionally limited: it demonstrates the arithmetic, not a recommendation. In a real decision, replace the illustrative values with documented figures and record the date, source and assumptions used.

How to use the calculator responsibly

  1. Choose the closest tool and read the input labels before entering values.
  2. Use consistent units and check whether the calculator expects a total, a rate, an average or a per-unit figure.
  3. Run a base case, a cautious case and a favourable case. A range usually communicates uncertainty better than a single number.
  4. Read the result alongside the formula and limitations. Save the assumptions if the estimate will be shared.

Interpretation and decision use

Plot the observations, check control behaviour and confirm that the units and specification limits refer to the same characteristic. Keep subgrouping and sampling notes. Use the output to ask a better question: what changes if demand falls, the input cost rises, the time period changes, or the measurement is off by a reasonable amount? Sensitivity is often more valuable than a false impression of precision. If two scenarios are close, the decision may depend on liquidity, safety, quality, tax treatment, service level or contractual terms that are outside this simple calculation.

Common mistakes to avoid

Using a changing process, mixing lots or using a biased measurement system can make the index misleading. Cp alone can hide an off-centre mean. Also check whether the calculator's denominator, date range and rounding convention match the document or process you are comparing. A result can be mathematically correct and still answer a different question from the one the user intended.

Review checklist for Cp and Cpk: A Practical Process Capability Explanation

A reliable way to use this guide is to treat the result as a documented estimate rather than a magic answer. First write the question in one sentence. Then list the inputs, their units, the date or period they describe, and the source of each important value. This small record makes it easier to notice when an input is stale, when two scenarios use different baselines, or when a result has been copied without its assumptions.

Next, run at least three cases. The base case should use the best-supported figures available. A cautious case should reflect a realistic downside such as a higher cost, lower output, slower payment, larger measurement or shorter useful period. A favourable case can show what would need to be true for the result to improve. Comparing the cases is more informative than reporting a single number with unnecessary decimal places.

When sharing the result, include the formula and the boundary of the calculation. Explain what is included and what is outside the model. A price estimate may exclude tax, delivery and fees. A production estimate may exclude downtime, rework and changeover. A health or finance estimate may not account for personal history, product terms or changing rules. The reader should be able to reproduce the arithmetic without guessing what the inputs meant.

  • Check that every input uses the unit shown beside the field.
  • Keep the original baseline visible when calculating a change or rate.
  • Do not round intermediate values unless the real process rounds at that stage.
  • Compare the output with an official document, product specification or qualified professional when the decision is regulated or safety-critical.
  • Save the date and assumptions when the estimate will be used later.
