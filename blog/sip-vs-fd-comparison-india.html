SIP vs Fixed Deposit: Compare Risk, Liquidity and Return Assumptions

Compare a market-linked SIP with a bank deposit without treating projected returns as guaranteed. · Published 13 November 2026

A systematic investment plan (SIP) and a fixed deposit (FD) are not interchangeable products. An SIP is a way to invest a fixed amount periodically into a mutual-fund scheme; the value depends on the scheme's underlying investments and market prices. An FD is a deposit with a bank or eligible institution that pays interest under stated terms. Comparing only two percentage figures can hide important differences in risk, liquidity and tax.

1. Understand what each option is

A SIP automates periodic purchases of mutual-fund units. It does not guarantee a profit, protect the principal or fix the return. The investor's result depends on the fund, market performance, instalment dates, costs and holding period. An FD accepts a deposit for a specified period and pays interest according to the bank's terms. Its rate and payout method are generally known when the deposit is booked, subject to the product conditions and premature-closure rules.

2. Compare certainty and market risk

An FD provides a contractual interest rate for the agreed term, though the depositor still needs to assess institution risk, deposit-insurance coverage, reinvestment risk and liquidity restrictions. A mutual fund is market-linked. Equity funds can fluctuate substantially; debt funds also carry interest-rate, credit and liquidity risks. A SIP changes the timing of purchases but does not remove the risk of loss.

3. Do not compare an FD rate with an assumed SIP return as if both are promises

A bank's FD rate is a product term. A SIP calculator's assumed annual return is only an input used to create a mathematical projection. It is not the expected return of a named fund or a guaranteed result. Run several hypothetical rates rather than selecting one optimistic assumption. For a fair comparison, keep the contribution amount and time horizon consistent and account for taxes and costs separately.

4. Understand liquidity and exit conditions

Many open-ended mutual funds allow redemption on business days, but settlement time, exit loads, market conditions and scheme rules matter. An FD may allow premature closure with a penalty or a reduced interest rate; some deposits have restrictions. A tax-saving deposit may have a lock-in. Before choosing, check when you may need the money and what happens if you withdraw earlier than planned.

5. Consider inflation and purchasing power

The future rupee amount is not the same as future purchasing power. Inflation can reduce what a fixed maturity amount buys. A useful comparison asks whether the after-tax return is likely to keep pace with the cost of the goal, while recognising that future inflation and market returns are uncertain. 6. Understand tax treatment

FD interest is generally taxable under applicable income-tax rules, and banks may deduct TDS when statutory conditions are met. Mutual-fund taxation depends on the scheme category, holding period, transaction date and current law. Do not assume that all mutual funds receive the same tax treatment. A post-tax comparison should use current official rules and the investor's overall tax circumstances.

7. Use calculators for separate estimates

The FD Calculator estimates deposit interest using a selected rate, tenure and compounding convention. The SIP Calculator estimates a future value using an assumed rate and instalment schedule. These tools answer different mathematical questions. They do not rank products, verify a fund's risk or determine whether an investment is suitable.

8. Match the tool to the goal

For a known near-term expense, certainty of amount and access date may matter more than an uncertain higher projection. For a long-term goal, some investors may consider market-linked assets as part of a diversified plan, while accepting that values can fall and recovery is not guaranteed. The right mix depends on the time horizon, ability to absorb losses, emergency reserves and financial circumstances. Do not put money needed soon into a volatile investment merely because a projection is higher.

9. A practical comparison checklist

  • Write down the date you need the money.
  • Compare the same total contribution or principal.
  • Check the FD's institution, rate, compounding and early-closure terms.
  • For a mutual fund, read the scheme information document, riskometer, portfolio and expense ratio.
  • Estimate taxes and inflation separately.
  • Keep emergency money accessible and avoid relying on a single return scenario.

10. Verify product details

For deposits, read the bank's current terms and check applicable DICGC coverage at the DICGC website. For mutual funds, review scheme documents and investor education material from AMFI and the relevant fund house. A qualified financial professional can help assess suitability. Frequently asked questions

Is an SIP safer than an FD?

No. An SIP is a method of investing in market-linked mutual funds and can lose value. An FD has different risks and contractual terms.

Are SIP calculator returns guaranteed?

No. The rate is a hypothetical input for a projection, not a promise or forecast.

Is FD interest tax-free?

FD interest is generally taxable subject to applicable rules. Check current tax law and your own circumstances.
