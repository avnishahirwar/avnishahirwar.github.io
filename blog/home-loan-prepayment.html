How to Repay a Home Loan Faster

Understand principal, interest, extra payments and lender rules

A home-loan EMI contains both interest and principal. Early in a typical amortising loan, a larger share of each EMI goes toward interest. An additional payment credited directly to principal can reduce the balance on which future interest is calculated. The effect depends on when the lender applies the payment and whether the EMI or remaining tenure is changed.

1. Understand the EMI formula

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here, P is the outstanding principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate divided by 12), and n is the number of remaining monthly instalments. This formula assumes a constant rate and regular monthly payments.

2. Worked illustration

Assume a loan of ₹30,00,000, an annual interest rate of 8.5% and a 20-year repayment period. The estimated EMI is about ₹26,035. If the rate stays unchanged for the full term, the total interest over the original schedule is approximately ₹32.48 lakh.

Now suppose an extra ₹1,00,000 is paid directly toward principal immediately after the 12th EMI, and the regular EMI remains unchanged. Under the same fixed-rate assumptions, the loan could finish about 18 months earlier, with estimated interest savings of roughly ₹3.70 lakh.

Illustration only: This is a mathematical estimate, not a lender quote. Actual savings vary with payment date, rate resets, lender allocation, fees and revised amortisation schedule.

3. A practical way to review prepayment

  1. Ask the lender for the current principal outstanding and an updated amortisation schedule.
  2. Confirm that the extra amount will be applied to principal, not treated as an advance EMI.
  3. Ask for two written options: keep EMI unchanged and reduce tenure, or reduce EMI and keep tenure similar.
  4. Compare the revised total interest and any applicable charges.
  5. Keep enough liquidity for regular expenses and unexpected needs before making a large extra payment.

4. RBI prepayment-charge directions

The Reserve Bank of India’s Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025 apply to loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026. They prohibit prepayment charges for specified floating-rate loans, including floating-rate loans granted to individuals for purposes other than business. The directions also cover certain loans to individuals and micro and small enterprises for business purposes. Applicability depends on the regulated lender, borrower, loan purpose, amount and whether a dual-rate loan is floating at the time of prepayment.

For eligible existing floating-rate term loans to individual borrowers sanctioned on or before 31 December 2025, the RBI directions also state that regulated entities must not levy prepayment charges. Fixed-rate loans and other cases may be treated differently. Read the applicable lender terms and the official RBI Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025.

6. Common mistakes

  • Assuming every extra transfer is automatically treated as principal prepayment.
  • Not checking whether the lender reduces EMI, tenure or lets you choose.
  • Ignoring floating-rate resets and the remaining loan term.
  • Using an online estimate instead of the lender's updated repayment schedule.
  • Overlooking liquidity needs or applicable contract terms.

Frequently asked questions

Does an extra payment reduce EMI or loan tenure?

That depends on the lender's recalculation options. If the EMI is kept unchanged, an extra principal payment can shorten the remaining tenure.

Are prepayment charges always zero?

RBI directions effective from 1 January 2026 restrict prepayment charges for specified floating-rate loans. Applicability depends on borrower, loan purpose, lender type, sanction or renewal date and rate type.

Should I reduce EMI or tenure after prepayment?

The result depends on cash flow and loan terms. Ask the lender for both revised schedules and compare total interest, liquidity and any charges.

Does this example match every home loan?

No. It is an illustration with a fixed rate and unchanged EMI. Actual schedules depend on lender calculations, rate resets, dates and contract terms.
