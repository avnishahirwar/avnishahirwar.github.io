How to Read a Loan Amortisation Schedule in India

Published by Friendly Calculator · Editorial contact: Avnish Ahirwar, Founder & Editor · Editorial policy

A loan amortisation schedule is a period-by-period view of how repayments reduce a loan balance. It helps explain why an EMI is not simply a fixed amount of principal, why interest is often a larger share of early instalments, and how a rate or tenure change can affect the remaining cost. It is useful when reviewing a home, personal, vehicle or other standard reducing-balance loan—but it is not a substitute for the lender's contract or Key Facts Statement (KFS).

Quick example (illustrative only)

Assume a fully disbursed loan of ₹10,00,000, a fixed nominal annual interest rate of 10%, monthly instalments and a 5-year tenure. A standard reducing-balance formula gives an EMI of about ₹21,247. Across 60 instalments, the total paid is about ₹12.75 lakh, of which about ₹2.75 lakh is interest before separate charges. Actual lender figures may differ because of dates, rounding, fees and contract terms.

1. What each column means

  • Instalment number or due date: identifies when a payment is scheduled. The first period may be shorter or longer than a standard month if disbursal and due dates differ.
  • Opening balance: principal outstanding immediately before the instalment is applied.
  • Interest component: interest charged for the period under the lender's method and rate.
  • Principal component: the part of the instalment that reduces the amount borrowed.
  • EMI / instalment amount: the scheduled payment. For a conventional fixed-rate amortising loan, it is usually principal plus interest; separate charges may be collected independently.
  • Closing balance: remaining principal after the payment is applied.

2. How the principal and interest split works

For a standard monthly reducing-balance estimate, periodic interest is calculated on the opening principal balance. Principal repaid is the instalment less that period's interest. The closing balance becomes the next period's opening balance.

Monthly interest = Opening balance × (Annual nominal rate ÷ 12)
Principal repaid = EMI − Monthly interest
Closing balance = Opening balance − Principal repaid

This simplified method assumes a constant rate and equal monthly periods. A lender may use actual day counts, specific payment dates, different disbursal conventions or rounding at each step. Use the lender's schedule for contractual amounts.

3. Why early instalments often contain more interest

At the start of a reducing-balance loan, outstanding principal is highest. Interest for that period is therefore calculated on a larger balance. As principal is repaid, the balance falls and the interest component usually becomes smaller. If the EMI remains unchanged, a larger share of later instalments goes toward principal. This is a normal feature of a fully amortising loan, not automatically evidence of an error.

4. How to compare two loan schedules

Do not compare only the EMI. Compare the amount actually disbursed, total scheduled repayments, total interest, all fees, insurance or bundled products, rate type, reset frequency, prepayment conditions and the first instalment due date. A lower EMI may reflect a longer tenure, which can increase total interest. A low advertised rate may exclude fees that affect the overall borrowing cost.

For covered new retail and MSME term loans, RBI's Key Facts Statement framework requires standardised disclosure including an APR computation sheet and amortisation schedule. Read the KFS and compare APR and charges, not just the headline rate. See the RBI circular on Key Facts Statement for Loans and Advances and the relevant lender's current terms.

5. What changes when the rate is floating

A floating rate can change after a benchmark or spread reset. Depending on the contract and lender process, the effect may appear as a change in EMI, remaining tenure or both. A schedule prepared at the original rate is therefore a scenario, not a permanent promise. When the lender communicates a reset, request the revised schedule and check the outstanding balance, new rate, revised payment and remaining term.

6. How part-prepayment appears

A part-prepayment is an extra amount applied to reduce principal, subject to the loan agreement and applicable rules. Ask the lender how the payment will be allocated and whether it will reduce the EMI, shorten the tenure or allow a choice. Request a revised schedule after the transaction. Do not assume every lender applies the payment on the date you initiate it or that there are no charges or conditions.

7. Common mistakes when reading a schedule

  • Assuming the entire EMI reduces principal.
  • Comparing loans using the interest rate alone and ignoring APR and charges.
  • Forgetting that a longer tenure can increase total interest even when EMI is lower.
  • Assuming a floating-rate schedule will remain unchanged for the full term.
  • Ignoring the actual disbursal date, first due date, part-disbursements or overdue amounts.
  • Treating a generic calculator output as a lender-issued repayment promise.

8. Use a calculator as a scenario tool

Enter principal, annual rate and tenure into the Loan EMI Calculator to estimate the regular payment and review the amortisation table. Change one input at a time to understand the effect of a shorter tenure or different rate. For home-loan scenarios, use the Home Loan EMI Calculator and compare extra-payment assumptions separately. Limitations and when to seek help

This article explains a standard reducing-balance structure. It does not model every lender convention, moratorium, step-up or step-down repayment, irregular disbursal, overdue interest, penal charge, insurance premium or restructuring arrangement. If the schedule conflicts with the loan agreement or statement, ask the lender for a written reconciliation. For a significant borrowing decision, consider advice from a qualified professional who can review the actual documents and your circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Does every EMI contain principal and interest?

For a standard fully amortising loan, each scheduled instalment includes both components, but their proportions change over time.

Why does the outstanding balance fall slowly at first?

The opening balance is largest at the start, so periodic interest is also relatively large. Less of the fixed instalment then reduces principal.

Is the EMI schedule the same as APR?

No. The schedule shows the timing and composition of repayments. APR expresses the annual cost of credit, including applicable charges under the relevant framework.

Can the lender's schedule differ from a calculator?

Yes. Actual dates, day-count conventions, rate resets, fees, rounding and disbursal details can change the lender's schedule.
